Ada Lovelace

Augusta Ada King, Countess of Lovelace10 December 1815 – 27 November 1852

Analyst (& Metaphysician)her own description

“We may say most aptly, that the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves.”

Note A · Scientific Memoirs III, 1843, p. 696 · signed A.A.L.

Her best-known work is a translation with notes. Seven appendices, signed A.A.L., turned a short memoir on Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine into the most searching account of what such a machine could become. This portfolio shows the originals, all in the public domain, beside new programs that run them.

Woven on this page by the loom in Exhibit II from pascal(x + y, y, 3), mirrored: one punched card per row of cloth.

Exhibits

Five programs

Exhibit I

The Bernoulli program

Note G · Scientific Memoirs III (1843), pp. 722–731, with the fold-out diagram

Note G ends with a complete program. Lovelace sets out, card by card, how the Analytical Engine would compute B7, the fourth Number of Bernoulli in her numbering (B8 today), when B1, B3 and B5 are already known. Each Variable V is a column of figure-discs in the Store. Each operation brings two columns to the Mill and sends the result back to one or more columns. The small number to the upper left of a V counts how many values that column has held, a notation she introduces in Note D.

The emulator below follows her table exactly and keeps every value as an exact fraction. The discs show the decimal figures the Engine itself would hold.

Lovelace notes that the same 25 cards compute every Number in turn, each result becoming data for the next: “a cycle of a cycle.” This runs her program for n = 1 to 10 and keeps each result, with its sign, for the next pass.

Card 7 is a test

Card 7 computes n − 1. If it is zero, B1 is finished and the Engine jumps to card 24. Lovelace explains that the test is needed because the same cards must serve every n. Cards 12 and 23 test again.

Cards 13–23 repeat

The cycle adds one more term each time it runs. For n = 4 it runs twice, so 25 cards perform 36 operations. Only card 21 changes between passes: it reads B3 from V22, then B5 from V23.

Card 4, as printed

The 1843 table prints card 4 as 2V5 ÷ 2V4, which divides 2n + 1 by 2n − 1. The statement beside it gives the intended (2n − 1)/(2n + 1). Tick the box to run the card as printed. Whether Lovelace, Babbage or the printer made the slip is not known.

Exhibit II

The algebraical loom

Note A, p. 696 · Note C · Note F

Jacquard’s loom reads a chain of punched cards. Each hole lets one warp thread lift, so the card decides which threads show on that row of cloth. Babbage borrowed the cards to direct the Analytical Engine, and Lovelace saw that the borrowing ran deeper than mechanism: in both machines the cards carry a pattern that is independent of what is being made.

Here the comparison runs the other way. Write an algebraic formula, and the page punches one card per row from it: a hole wherever the formula is not zero. The loom then weaves.

“There is in existence a beautiful woven portrait of Jacquard, in the fabrication of which 24,000 cards were required.”Note F, 1843

Variables: x thread, y row, m. Operators: + − * / % ^ and comparisons. Functions: pascal(a, b, p) is the binomial coefficient C(a, b) mod p; also abs, gcd, min, max. Mirror reads the first half of the chain again in reverse, like the “backing” of cards Lovelace describes in Note C.

Exhibit III

Pitched sounds

Note A, p. 694

“Supposing, for instance, that the fundamental relations of pitched sounds in the science of harmony and of musical composition were susceptible of such expression and adaptations, the engine might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any degree of complexity or extent.”Note A, 1843

The relations she names are ratios. A fifth is 3 : 2, a major third 5 : 4. This exhibit takes a sequence the Engine could compute, turns each figure into a degree of a justly tuned scale, and plays it over a steady drone. The pen draws each ratio as a harmonograph would, with two pendulums swinging at the two frequencies. Simple ratios close into simple figures.

Scale degrees 0–9, from the drone up: 1/1, 9/8, 5/4, 4/3, 3/2, 5/3, 15/8, 2/1, 9/4, 5/2. A gap in the roll is a rest between numbers.

Exhibit IV

The discovering faculty

Manuscript reflection, 5 January 1841

“Imagination is the Discovering Faculty, pre-eminently. It is that which penetrates into the unseen worlds around us, the worlds of Science.”5 January 1841 · quoted in Toole (1992)

Place N points round a circle. From each point i draw a straight chord to the point k × i, counting round the circle modulo N. Nothing else is drawn. At k = 2 a heart-shaped curve, the cardioid, appears where the chords crowd together; at k = 3, a kidney-shaped nephroid. No line traces either curve. They are envelopes, visible only once a multiplication table is laid out in space.

k = 2cardioid · 1 cusp

For whole numbers k, the envelope is an epicycloid with k − 1 cusps. Between whole numbers the figure is caught halfway from one curve to the next.

Exhibit V

Flyology, Plate I

Letters to Lady Byron, 1828

At twelve, Ada set out to fly. She studied the anatomy of birds, weighed paper, oilsilk, wire and feathers as materials for wings, and planned a book called Flyology with plates of her findings. This plate takes up one question her book would have had to answer: where does a wing actually go?

The wingtip beats up and down while the body moves forward, so in still air the tip draws a wave. The distance between crests is the ground covered in one wingbeat. Change the flight speed and the wingbeat to stretch or crowd the wave. The model is deliberately simple: a rigid wing hinged at the shoulder.

2.0 mforward per wingbeat

Essay

Poetical science

What she meant by the phrase, and why it still describes the Notes.

An inversion

The phrase survives in a letter to her mother, written in her thirties: “If you can’t give me poetry, can’t you give me ‘poetical science’?”[2] It reverses the plan of her upbringing. Lady Byron, whom Byron had called his “Princess of Parallelograms,” kept her daughter to a strict course of mathematics, partly to steer her away from her father’s temperament.[3] Ada never knew Byron. Her parents separated when she was a month old, and he died in Greece in 1824, when she was eight.

She did not choose between the two inheritances. She asked for a science that kept poetry’s ways of seeing, and she held herself to a mathematician’s standard while doing it. Her correspondence course with Augustus De Morgan shows her worrying at definitions and limits with the persistence of a good student, which is what she was.[4]

Imagination as an instrument

In January 1841 she called imagination “the Discovering Faculty, pre-eminently.” She did not mean fancy. She meant the faculty that combines things never seen together, and so finds relations the senses do not show. Exhibit IV is a small case: the multiplication table, laid round a circle, draws a curve that no one ruled.

A metaphor that became a design

In Note A the loom is more than an image. The point of the comparison is that cards carry a pattern independently of the material. The Analytical Engine, she writes, is “an embodying of the science of operations,” and so it “might act upon other things besides number,” provided their relations could be written in its notation. Music is her example.[1] The step from a calculator to a machine for any symbols whose rules can be written down is the passage historians of computing most often cite as her own contribution.

Rigour as care

The poetry does not excuse the arithmetic. Note G accounts for every operation and every value: which cards repeat, why card 7 must test n − 1, how the upper index of V11 runs 0, 1, 2, 0, 3. One slip survives in print, a transposed pair on card 4, and Exhibit I runs the table both ways. The slip does not touch the method. The statement printed beside the card is correct, and with the pair restored the program produces every Bernoulli number we asked of it, through B19 in her numbering.

What the Engine cannot do

Note G also sets a limit:

“The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform.”

In 1950 Alan Turing named this “Lady Lovelace’s Objection” and argued against it.[5] The rest of her paragraph is read less often. She expects the Engine to act back on mathematics: arranging its truths so that a machine can use them throws subjects “into new lights.” Every exhibit on this page is an instance of that second claim. None of them adds a theorem; each shows a familiar relation from an angle the formula alone does not give.

Coda

Babbage, writing to her in September 1843, called her “the Enchantress of Number.”[2] She died of cancer on 27 November 1852, at thirty-six, the age at which her father had died, and was buried beside him at Hucknall. In 1980 the United States Department of Defense named a programming language Ada. Its reference manual was numbered MIL-STD-1815, for the year of her birth.

Notes A–G at a glance

  1. AWhat the Engine is: the science of operations; the loom; music.
  2. BThe Store: numbers held on columns of discs, “probably at least two hundred.”
  3. CBacking the cards, so a set can be used again and again.
  4. DA computation traced in full; the upper indices explained.
  5. ECycles, and cycles of cycles, through a trigonometrical series.
  6. FHow repetition saves cards; the woven portrait of Jacquard.
  7. GThe Engine’s limits, and the Bernoulli numbers, card by card.

Chronology

A life in dates

  1. 1815

    Born 10 December in London, the only child of Lord Byron and Anne Isabella Milbanke.

  2. 1816

    Her parents separate. Byron leaves England in April and never sees her again.

  3. 1824

    Byron dies at Missolonghi, in Greece. Ada is eight.

  4. 1828

    Flyology: studies of birds and wings, and a plan for a flying machine.

  5. 1829

    A long illness after measles keeps her in bed for many months.

  6. 1833

    In June she meets Charles Babbage and soon sees the working fragment of his Difference Engine.

  7. 1835

    Marries William King. They have three children by 1839.

  8. 1838

    King is created Earl of Lovelace; she becomes Countess of Lovelace.

  9. 1840

    Begins studying calculus by correspondence with Augustus De Morgan.

  10. 1842

    October: L. F. Menabrea’s memoir on the Analytical Engine appears in Geneva, in French.

  11. 1843

    Her translation, with Notes A–G signed A.A.L., is published in Scientific Memoirs, vol. III.

  12. 1844

    Writes to Woronzow Greig of a “calculus of the nervous system.”

  13. 1852

    Sits for Henry Phillips. Dies 27 November, aged 36; buried beside her father at Hucknall.

  14. 1950

    Alan Turing answers “Lady Lovelace’s Objection” in Computing Machinery and Intelligence.

  15. 1980

    The Ada programming language is standardised as MIL-STD-1815.

  16. 2026

    This portfolio: her Note G program runs again, card for card.
